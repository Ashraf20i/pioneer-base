    Run a base on the Moon or Mars

    Four astronauts. Thirty days. You make the plan each day. Keep everyone safe and healthy until the mission ends.

    1. Plan the day. Switch systems on or off, pick what the crew does, and choose their food rations.
    2. Watch the numbers. Power, oxygen, water, food and radiation all pull against each other.
    3. Press Run day. At the end, the game shows the exact chain of choices that made your mission work or fail.

        Teacher guide: Pioneer Base

        A 45-minute lesson for ages 7 to 12. Students run a Moon or Mars outpost for 30 mission days and learn why real missions need trade-offs. No account, no install, and it works offline. One game takes 8 to 12 minutes.

        Learning goals

        • Explain that a space base needs power, oxygen, water, food and protection from radiation at the same time.
        • Describe one trade-off, such as spending crates on a reactor instead of a greenhouse.
        • Tell the difference between a Real number, a Derived number and a Simplified number, and say why a model simplifies.
        • Read a cause chain and name the first decision or event that started a failure.

        45-minute lesson outline

        TimeWhat happens
        0-5 minHook. Ask: "If you lived on the Moon, what would you need every day?" List answers on the board. Point out air, water, food, power and protection.
        5-10 minDemo. Show the briefing screen. Play day 1 together on a projector. Read the Mission Control hint aloud. Show the "Today's preview" box.
        10-25 minPlay 1. Pairs play one full mission on the site they choose. Pairs write down one decision they are proud of and one they regret.
        25-35 minDebrief. Each pair reads their cause chain to another pair. Ask: which step could you have changed? Which crate would you spend differently?
        35-42 minPlay 2. Pairs try again, or switch to the other site, with one new plan.
        42-45 minWrap-up. Open "Where do these numbers come from?". Ask which numbers are Real and which are Simplified. Discuss question 5 below.

        Five discussion questions

        1. Why is a reactor useful on the Moon but a bigger battery is not enough? (The lunar night lasts about 15 Earth days.)
        2. Shelter protects the crew in a solar storm, but the crew does no science that day. When is that worth it?
        3. The greenhouse costs power and water, and crops take 10 days in the game. Why do real crops take even longer?
        4. Why does the game show "runs out in N days"? How do real mission controllers use the same idea?
        5. The game simplifies many numbers. Which simplification is fair, and which one might give a wrong idea?

        Answers and notes for the teacher

        • The Moon has no air, so radiation is a steady danger. Without shield layers a full 30-day Moon mission goes over the game's 40 mSv limit. NASA's real career limit is 600 mSv, and the game shows both.
        • On Mars, dust slowly covers the solar arrays. A Maintenance day cleans them. The dust storm cuts solar power for 6 days, so a reactor or a charged battery helps.
        • The ISS recycles about 98% of its water. The game outpost recycles 90%, and that number is tagged Simplified.
        • The "Myth or Reality?" cards between days each name their source. Ask students to say Myth or Reality before they tap.
        • Failure in the game is always an evacuation, never worse. Failing is part of the lesson. Encourage a second try.

        Tips

        • Works on laptops, tablets and phones. Use keyboard Tab and Space or Enter if a mouse is not available.
        • To keep the lesson short, tell students the hints in days 1 to 3 are on purpose, and that they can close them.
